## Section 2-201.12(A)(1) — Exclusion Requirement

According to FDA Food Code 2026, Section 2-201.12(A)(1) states that **except when the symptom is from a noninfectious condition**, the person in charge must **EXCLUDE** a food employee who is symptomatic with **vomiting or diarrhea**. In other words, if a food employee has vomiting or diarrhea and there is no known noninfectious cause, the person in charge must exclude that employee from working.

This requirement is based on the public health rationale that it is necessary to exclude food employees symptomatic with diarrhea, vomiting, or jaundice, or suffering from a disease likely to be transmitted through contamination of food (Annex 3, page 383).

## Section 2-201.13(A)(1) — Reinstatement Criteria

Section 2-201.13(A)(1) addresses when an excluded food employee may be reinstated. Based on the available text, reinstatement requires that the excluded or restricted food employee **provides to the person in charge written medical documentation from a health practitioner** (the provision continues with additional conditions). The employee must also obtain clearance from the regulatory authority.

## Additional Context

- The person in charge must also **prohibit a conditional employee** who reports a listed symptom (including vomiting or diarrhea) from becoming a food employee until meeting the criteria in Section 2-201.13 (page 387).
- Table 1a in Annex 3 provides a summary of requirements for symptomatic food employees, distinguishing between facilities serving a high-risk population (HSP) and those not serving an HSP (page 388).

## Immediate Actions When a Food Employee Begins Vomiting

### What the Employee Should Do (per the Employee Health and Personal Hygiene Handbook, p. 6):

If vomiting occurs **at work**, the employee must:

1. **Stop work immediately;**
2. **Report to management; and**
3. **Go home** and return after at least **24 hours** have passed since the vomiting and diarrhea symptoms ended.

### What the Manager Should Do:

The handbook (p. 7) addresses what a manager should do when a food employee reports symptoms. The manager must ensure the symptomatic employee is **excluded** from the operation (p. 31 confirms that vomiting triggers **exclusion**). The Person in Charge (PIC) must also consider:

- Whether the employee is asymptomatic
- Whether medical documentation is required
- Whether regulatory authority (RA) approval is needed for the employee to return to work (p. 11)

### Earliest Return to Work:

The employee may return **after at least 24 hours have passed since the vomiting and diarrhea symptoms ended** (p. 6). 

However, if the vomiting is linked to a confirmed diagnosis of STEC (Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli*), the return-to-work criteria are more stringent: symptoms must have resolved **and** more than **30 days** must have passed since the employee became asymptomatic, with RA approval required (p. 32).

### Summary Table:

| Scenario | Earliest Return |
|----------|----------------|
| Vomiting (general) | ≥ 24 hours after symptoms end |
| Confirmed STEC | Symptoms resolved + >30 days asymptomatic + RA approval |

|-----------|--------------------------|-------------------|
| **Minimum water activity (a_w)** | 0.92 | 0.94 |

### Key Takeaways:

- **Water activity:** *Listeria monocytogenes* can grow at a slightly lower water activity (0.92) compared to *Salmonella* (0.94), meaning *Listeria* can tolerate slightly drier conditions.
- **pH range:** *Salmonella* can grow at a lower minimum pH (3.7 vs. 4.4), making it more acid-tolerant on the low end. *Listeria* has a slightly narrower upper range (9.4 vs. 9.5).
- **Temperature range:** *Listeria monocytogenes* can grow at much lower temperatures (down to −0.4°C, i.e., just below freezing), whereas *Salmonella* requires a minimum of 5.2°C. Both have similar upper limits (~45–46°C).
- **Salt tolerance:** *Listeria* tolerates a higher maximum salt concentration (10%) than *Salmonella* (8%).
- **Oxygen requirement:** Both are facultative anaerobes, meaning they can grow with or without oxygen.

Overall, *Listeria monocytogenes* has a broader overall growth envelope (lower a_w, lower temperature, higher salt tolerance), while *Salmonella* is more acid-tolerant at the low pH end.

Query Text:

A food employee with no diagnosis and no known noninfectious condition vomited
while preparing ready-to-eat salads in a nursing home. Using the FDA Food Code
2026 and the *Bad Bug Book*, explain the required employee disposition, the
earliest conditions for return, and why possible norovirus transmission is a
concern. Treat vomiting as a symptom; the scenario supplies no norovirus
diagnosis.

Status: response_generated

Response:
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
## Required Employee Disposition

Under the **FDA Food Code 2026, Section 2-201.12(A)(1)** (page 59), a food employee who is **symptomatic with vomiting** must be **excluded** from the food establishment—unless the symptom is attributable to a noninfectious condition. Since the scenario explicitly states there is no known noninfectious condition and no diagnosis, **exclusion is mandatory**. The Person in Charge (PIC) is responsible for enforcing this requirement (page 420).

Because a **nursing home** serves a **Highly Susceptible Population (HSP)**, the exclusion requirement applies with the most stringent interpretation (page 31 of the Employee Health Handbook; page 405 of the Food Code).

---

## Earliest Conditions for Return

The FDA Food Code 2026 provides return pathways depending on the situation:

1. **Symptom-based return (no diagnosis):** The excluded employee may return when:
   - Written medical documentation from a health care practitioner states the employee is free of the condition, **or**
   - The regulatory authority determines the employee is no longer infectious (page 63).

2. **If norovirus is later confirmed or suspected (HSP facility):** Per Section 2-201.13(B)(1)(a) (page 66), **more than 48 hours** must have passed since the last day the employee was symptomatic. In HSP settings (page 405), additional regulatory authority approval may be required before return.

3. **Asymptomatic shedding concern:** The Employee Health and Personal Hygiene Handbook (page 28) notes that a food employee can be an **asymptomatic shedder of norovirus** and should remain excluded until medically cleared or for at least 24 hours from the date of diagnosis—underscoring that symptom resolution alone may not eliminate transmission risk.

The Employee Health Handbook's Table (page 31) confirms that for vomiting, **RA (Regulatory Authority) approval is required to return to work** in HSP facilities.

---

## Why Norovirus Transmission Is a Concern

Several factors converge to make this scenario particularly dangerous:

| Factor | Explanation |
|--------|-------------|
| **Vomiting is a hallmark norovirus symptom** | The PIC must consider norovirus as a probable etiology when vomiting occurs with no other explanation (page 420). |
| **Ready-to-eat salads** | No "kill step" (cooking) follows the employee's handling, so any viral contamination is directly ingested by consumers. |
| **High infectivity** | The exclusion rationale is based on "high infectivity and/or severe medical consequences" (page 384). Norovirus requires very few viral particles to cause illness. |
| **Asymptomatic shedding** | An employee can shed virus even after symptoms resolve (page 28), meaning the infectious period may extend well beyond visible illness. |
| **Highly Susceptible Population** | Nursing home residents (elderly, immunocompromised) face severe outcomes including dehydration, hospitalization, and death from norovirus. |

---

## Summary

| Element | Requirement |
|---------|-------------|
| **Disposition** | Exclusion (mandatory in HSP facility) |
| **Earliest return** | ≥48 hours symptom-free + medical clearance + RA approval (HSP) |
| **Key risk** | Norovirus transmission via RTE food to vulnerable residents, with no kill step and potential for asymptomatic shedding |

The combination of a symptomatic employee, a no-kill-step food vehicle, and a highly susceptible consumer population makes immediate exclusion and rigorous return criteria essential public health controls.

The **2026 FDA Food Code** should control any answer about the current FDA model-code rule for a vomiting employee. Use the **2026 Food Code** (particularly § 2-201.12 and Annex 3, Table 1a) as the authoritative source for the current exclusion/restriction rule for vomiting employees.
